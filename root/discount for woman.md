- ## Why 50% when the average pay gap is ~18%?
- Because the tech sector needs urgent acceleration—and women are the missing multiplier.
- Globally, women earn about 83 cents for every dollar men earn—an average unadjusted gender pay gap of around 17–18% (EqualPayToday 2026, OECD 2025 averages). Yet in the tech and innovation ecosystem—the very engine driving us toward Type I civilization—the underrepresentation is far more acute.
- Women make up only 24–28% of the global tech/STEM workforce in 2025 (WomenTech Network, Lemon.io, CompTIA State of the Tech Workforce 2025, Society of Women Engineers). In core computing, engineering, and software roles, the figure often drops to 24–26%. This isn't just unfair—it's inefficient. Tech desperately needs more diverse talent to solve planetary-scale problems: AI alignment, energy mastery, existential risk mitigation, and cross-disciplinary breakthroughs.
- At major tech conferences and events, the gender imbalance is stark: women typically comprise only 20–35% of attendees and speakers (e.g., ~32.6% female speakers at major tech events in 2024–2025 data from Pomona Partners and similar audits; many general tech gatherings hover closer to 20–30% female participation). The classic 80/20 male-to-female split persists in many spaces, reinforcing barriers and myths that tech is "male-coded," overly complex, or unwelcoming.
- At burn.city, we reject that narrative.
- Among women, there are already vast reserves of scientific and technological talent: women earn ~35–36% of STEM degrees globally (UNESCO, various 2025 reports), represent ~27–30% of the broader STEM workforce in many regions, and excel in fields like biotechnology, health sciences, and emerging interdisciplinary areas. History and the present are full of trailblazing women—Marie Curie, Ada Lovelace, Grace Hopper, Hedy Lamarr, Katherine Johnson—who pioneered breakthroughs that shaped the modern world. And today, we see brilliant living examples: Sabrina Gonzalez Pasterski, the theoretical physicist dubbed the "next Einstein" for her independent work on quantum gravity and holography (she built her own airplane at 14, graduated top of MIT, and was cited by Stephen Hawking); and Anita Williams Woolley, whose research at Carnegie Mellon demonstrated that collective intelligence in groups rises significantly with greater female participation—because teams with more women show higher social sensitivity, better collaboration, and superior problem-solving on complex tasks (as shown in her influential 2010 studies published in Science and PNAS).
- The talent exists; the welcoming, high-leverage environment often doesn't.
- We are building exactly that environment at burn.city: a space for comfortable, fearless creativity, amplified by cross-disciplinary tools and existential questions that define our era (AI safety, long-termism, civilizational resilience, abundance engineering). We're especially interested in women who are driven to explore the new, the uncertain, and the potentially highly rewarding—those ready to turn rational thinking into scalable impact.
- After all, agree with us: the scientific method and a rational mindset have never bankrupted anyone—in fact, they've done quite the opposite.
- Our 50% discount for women is bold by design: it's not just closing an 18% gap—it's overcorrecting to create massive incentive, lower barriers to entry, and signal that burn.city is serious about accelerating female participation in the tech redesign of civilization. More women in the room means faster progress toward Type I.
